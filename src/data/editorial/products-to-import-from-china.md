# Importing Products from China: Selection Logic and Trade-offs

Choose products to import from China by checking demand, landed cost, compliance, quality specifications and supplier alternatives. Use category lists as research starting points, then test a small order before scaling.

*Prepared from the supplied editorial draft; selected policy statements reviewed on 10 November 2026. Product-specific rules require confirmation with the relevant authority before ordering and shipment.*


## Who this guide is for

This guide is for importers who are choosing a first product, or reviewing a product they already want to sell, and who need to understand the full cost and compliance picture before placing an order. It covers the United States, the European Union, Australia, and the Middle East. The core sections apply everywhere. The market sections explain what differs by destination.

It does not tell you which product will make money. No honest guide can. What it can do is give you a method for deciding, and a checklist for not making expensive mistakes.


Buying gifts or items for personal use? The [China shopping guide](/guides/what-to-buy-in-china/) covers visitor purchases, price references and packing considerations.

## Part 1: Core principles (market-neutral)

### There is no universally best product to import

Product lists circulate widely: "best-selling," "most profitable," "trending." Treat them as sources of ideas, not rankings. A product that sells well for one seller can lose money for another because of different prices, channels, order sizes, return rates, or compliance costs.

### What a good first product looks like

A suitable first product usually meets most of these conditions:

1. **Demand shows up in several independent places.** Look for search interest, marketplace reviews on competing listings, and actual sales signals from more than one channel. One trending post is not evidence.
2. **It is economical to ship.** Weight and volume relative to value matter more than most beginners expect. A product that is cheap to make but bulky can be unprofitable at small volumes.
3. **Compliance is manageable for your target market.** Some products need certifications, test reports, labelling, or registrations before they can legally be sold. Factor these in before you fall in love with a product.
4. **Quality can be checked against a written specification.** If you cannot describe what "good" means in measurable terms (dimensions, materials, capacity, drop test, cycle count), your inspection will be guesswork.
5. **There is room to differentiate.** Colour, packaging, bundle, or a clear functional improvement can help you avoid competing purely on price.
6. **Several capable suppliers exist.** Depending on one factory is a risk. Make sure you can find alternatives.
7. **You can test it with a small order.** Your first order should be small enough that a failure is a lesson, not a disaster.

### Calculate the full landed cost, not the factory price

Profitability depends on the full cost from factory to customer. At minimum, include:

- Product price
- Packaging, including inserts, cartons, and any retail packaging
- China-side transport from factory to port or consolidation point
- Ocean or air freight
- Cargo insurance
- Import duties and tariffs
- Import taxes such as VAT or GST, where applicable (note that some are recoverable, depending on your registration)
- Customs brokerage and port charges
- Product testing and certification
- Domestic delivery to your warehouse or customer
- Warehousing and fulfilment
- Marketplace or payment fees
- Returns and refunds
- Currency conversion and payment costs
- Product liability insurance and a recall reserve, if your product is in a regulated category

Calculate profit at several selling prices, and under several duty scenarios: current rates, a higher rate, and a loss of an exemption you currently rely on. A product that only works under optimistic assumptions is a weak candidate.

This guide does not quote margins, freight prices, or inspection fees. These depend on product, channel, price, order size, season, and duty, and any figure published here would be out of date quickly.

### Build a specification before you request a quote

Write down what you are buying in measurable terms: materials, dimensions, weight, capacity, colour references, performance targets, and the tests the product must pass. Attach this to your purchase order. A written specification is the basis for quality inspection, for resolving disputes, and for comparing suppliers fairly.

### Start small and test

Make a first order that is large enough to sell and learn from, and small enough that a mistake will not sink the business. Expect the first order to teach you more about packaging, labelling, and customer expectations than about the factory.


## Part 2: Ten product categories for research

The ten categories below are presented as research starting points. Each has its own trade-offs in weight, compliance, customisation, and sourcing. Sourcing regions are described as typical clusters and should be confirmed with suppliers and your own visits.

1. **Phone and small electronics accessories.** Compact and easy to ship, but very competitive, with high trademark and counterfeit risk and significant compliance requirements (see Part 4).
2. **Home, kitchen, and drinkware.** Broad range and good sourcing depth. Food-contact materials and claims about insulation or leak-proofing need verification.
3. **Pet products.** Strong repeat demand. Safe materials are essential, especially for chew items, and powered products add electrical and battery requirements.
4. **Fitness and wellness accessories.** Simple, non-powered products have low entry barriers. Powered massage or therapy devices carry higher regulatory risk, and medical-type claims can move a product into a regulated category.
5. **Fashion accessories and bags.** Fast-changing styles and large numbers of suppliers. Watch for design and trademark issues, and for textile labelling rules in some markets.
6. **Beauty tools and hair accessories.** Beauty tools are not the same as cosmetics, but skincare or medical claims can bring them under cosmetics rules. Brush materials may require declarations in some markets.
7. **Toys, stationery, and party goods.** Children's products have the heaviest compliance burden in most markets. Chemical, small-parts, and mechanical safety testing, plus age labelling, all matter.
8. **Seasonal decor, gifts, and string lights.** Strong seasonal peaks, so timing is critical. Powered lighting requires electrical safety certification in most markets.
9. **Furniture and bulky goods.** High freight cost relative to value, low tolerance for returns, and sample-versus-production mismatch is a common problem. Formaldehyde and VOC limits apply in several markets.
10. **Fabrics and trims.** Dye lot variation is normal in textiles. Confirm whether your supplier weaves and dyes, or is a trader, and agree on an acceptable dye-lot tolerance in writing.

Planning to compare samples in person? Start with the [Yiwu district and product-floor map](/guides/yiwu-market-map/) for small commodities, and confirm each shop’s minimum order before visiting.

### Where to find each category

Sourcing is usually split between two kinds of place, and many importers confuse them:

- **Trading markets** (wholesale malls and showrooms) are good for looking at samples, comparing prices, and placing small trial orders. A stall may be a trader, wholesaler or factory sales office; its location does not establish who manufactures the goods.
- **Manufacturing clusters** (industrial towns and districts) are where large or custom orders, mould development, and certified production are usually handled.

Many cities host both. Some markets sit in one city while the factories are in surrounding towns. When you visit, ask for the factory address and, where possible, visit it. Trading and manufacturing are both legitimate, but you should know which one you are dealing with.

Examples of common patterns in China's sourcing landscape include:

- Consumer electronics: trading in Shenzhen, manufacturing in Shenzhen's outer districts and neighbouring Dongguan.
- Household goods and drinkware: trading in Yiwu, manufacturing across Zhejiang.
- Lighting and seasonal goods: trading in Yiwu, manufacturing in Zhongshan.
- Furniture: showrooms in Foshan's Lecong area, manufacturing in Longjiang, Shunde.
- Toys: trading in Yiwu, large-scale manufacturing in Shantou's Chenghai district.
- Bags: wholesale in Guangzhou, manufacturing in Huadu's Shiling area.
- Fabrics: trading in Shaoxing Keqiao, with weaving and dyeing nearby.

Verify each cluster before you rely on it. Industrial patterns shift.


## Part 3: Order process and the calendar

### Payment structure

Avoid paying the full amount upfront. A common structure for small and mid-size importers is a deposit, with the balance paid after inspection and before shipment. Be cautious of suppliers who demand full prepayment or who quote prices far below the market.

Be clear about trade terms. Under FOB and similar terms, make sure the contract states who pays terminal handling, documentation, and port-related charges. Many disputes come from these smaller items, not from the main price.

### Inspection and acceptance

Inspection is most useful when the acceptance criteria are written into the purchase contract before production. Agree on:

- The Acceptable Quality Level (AQL) and the defect categories
- Functional tests the product must pass, such as power-on, charge cycle, drop, or leak test
- Battery capacity measurement, where batteries are involved
- Packaging and label checks
- Who pays for re-inspection if the first inspection fails

Inspection can be carried out by you, by a representative, or by a third-party inspection firm. Some importers attend the inspection in person, especially for a first order or a high-value product. This is a reasonable choice and gives you direct knowledge of the product.

### Sample, pilot, bulk

A practical sequence is:

1. Source and compare samples.
2. Confirm specifications in writing.
3. Test samples yourself or through an accredited lab.
4. Place a pilot order with neutral labelling, if possible.
5. Inspect the pilot run against the agreed AQL.
6. Scale up with custom packaging only once the product has sold.

A full custom packaging run on a first order raises the cost of a mistake. Consider neutral packaging for the pilot and move to custom packaging once demand is confirmed.

### The Spring Festival calendar

Chinese New Year, which falls in February in 2027, affects the whole supply chain, not only factories. Based on operating experience, the pattern is:

- Factories often stop work well before the official public holiday, typically about 7 to 10 days before, and restart later than the holiday ends, typically 3 to 7 days after.
- Logistics providers, trucking firms, port operations, and warehouses slow down on the same timetable, and some close entirely for part of the period.
- Suppliers, sub-suppliers, and packaging printers each follow their own schedules, so a delay at any one of them can hold up the whole order.

These are operating experience, not official rules. Real timing varies by supplier, product, and region. Plan a buffer of at least three weeks around the holiday, and confirm dates with each supplier and forwarder in writing.

### Seasonal timing

Seasonal products need to arrive well before the sales window. For holiday lighting, decor, and gifts, many importers plan arrival at least 60 days before the season, and earlier for large orders or for products that need certification. Late arrivals often mean stock that cannot be sold at full price.


## Part 4: Compliance: what to check in every market

Compliance is where most first-time importers lose money. The key principle is that **different certifications serve different purposes and do not replace one another.** A safety test for shipping lithium batteries does not authorise a product for sale. A European product standard does not satisfy an American one.

Check these categories whatever your destination:

- **Test reports must match the finished product.** A raw-material report is not a report on the finished item. A report on an earlier version becomes invalid if the material, formula, or design changes.
- **Verify certificates with the issuing body.** Do not rely on a PDF from a supplier. Check the report number, the laboratory's accreditation, and the scope of the test.
- **Marketplace rules can be stricter than law.** A platform may require certificates or documents that the law does not, and may delist a product even if customs has cleared it.
- **Labelling and language requirements differ by market.** Age warnings, material content, country of origin, and safety information may all need to appear in a particular language and format.
- **Intellectual property applies beyond logos.** Design patents, trade dress, and copyright can all be grounds for seizure or delisting, even when no brand name appears on the product.
- **HS classification is your responsibility.** The same product can fall under different tariff codes with very different duty rates. Do not rely solely on a supplier's classification. Where possible, request a binding ruling from the customs authority of your importing country.
- **Keep records.** Retain test reports, purchase contracts, commercial invoices, and certificates for the period your regulator requires.

The sections below describe the main considerations for each market. They are starting points for your own verification, not legal advice. Confirm each item with the official source at the time of import.


## Part 5: Market modules

### Module A: United States

**Parcel and low-value entry rules.** The US de minimis exemption, which allowed low-value shipments to enter duty-free, has been suspended. As verified on 9 November 2026, the suspension applies to both non-postal shipments (express, sea, and air) and postal shipments. This means low-value goods now enter subject to duties and must be declared. Confirm the current CBP guidance before you plan a parcel-based business, because the rules have changed several times since 2025.

**Tariffs.** Establish the product classification, origin and entry date before calculating duties. Ask your broker to identify every applicable duty and exclusion using the current HTSUS, USTR notices and CBP guidance. Do not use a single headline tariff percentage as the landed-cost assumption.

**Section 301 exclusions.** A set of product exclusions from Section 301 duties has been subject to extension decisions. Exclusions have mostly covered industrial and capital goods, not finished consumer products. Confirm the current expiry date and the product codes covered in the Federal Register or USTR notices before relying on any exclusion.

**Forced labour and seizure risk.** The Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act allows US Customs to detain goods where there is reason to believe they were produced with forced labour. Detention can happen even when duties have been paid. Keep supply-chain documentation for materials and components, especially for products that use silicone, textiles, electronics components, or other materials that appear in enforcement actions. Shipments may be held while you respond to requests for information, so plan for delay.

**Certifications, by product type:**

- **Wireless products (Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, and similar):** FCC authorisation. Check the FCC ID in the FCC database; fake or copied IDs are common. UN 38.3 battery transport testing is a shipping requirement and does not replace FCC authorisation.
- **Children's products:** Children's Product Certificate (CPC) based on testing by a CPSC-accepted laboratory, and permanent tracking labels on the product or packaging. The importer is typically responsible for the certificate, so supplier documents alone are not enough.
- **General consumer products:** A General Certificate of Conformity (GCC) may be required for products covered by specific CPSC rules. Check whether your product category is covered.
- **Electrical products:** Many retailers and marketplaces require UL or another recognised certification, even where federal law does not. Confirm with your sales channel before you invest.
- **Food-contact materials:** FDA requirements apply to materials that contact food. Confirm the material and intended use.
- **Cosmetics and personal care:** Products making skincare or cosmetic claims fall under cosmetics rules. Medical or therapeutic claims can bring a product under medical device rules.
- **Labelling:** California Proposition 65 warnings may apply where listed chemicals are present, and retailers may ask for compliance documents.

### Module B: European Union

**Parcel and low-value rules.** EU guidance describes a temporary €3 customs duty per item for qualifying low-value imports from 1 July 2026. It is not simply a charge per parcel. The separate Union handling fee has different rules; confirm its current amount, scope and start date rather than assuming a €2 charge. See the [European Commission guidance](https://taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu/news/guidance-and-legal-text-temporary-flat-fee-low-value-imports-which-will-apply-until-1-july-2028-2026-06-08_en).

**B2B and bulk imports.** The parcel-level charges mainly affect business-to-consumer direct shipments. Full-container or palletised imports follow the normal tariff and clearance process, but confirm with your broker whether any new charges apply to your specific shipment type.

**VAT.** Import VAT is payable at entry in most cases. Whether you can recover it depends on your VAT registration and the country of import. Plan cash flow accordingly.

**Core product rules:**

- **Radio equipment:** Products with radio functions must comply with the Radio Equipment Directive (RED) and carry CE marking. US FCC authorisation does not satisfy the EU requirement.
- **Electrical safety:** The Low Voltage Directive (LVD) and the Electromagnetic Compatibility (EMC) rules apply to many powered products.
- **General product safety:** The General Product Safety Regulation (GPSR) applies to consumer products, including requirements for traceability and information on the product or packaging.
- **Chemicals:** REACH restrictions apply, including nickel release limits for jewellery and some accessories. Testing total metal content alone is often not sufficient; check whether the test measures nickel release, which is the relevant measure for skin-contact items.
- **RoHS:** Restrictions on hazardous substances in electrical and electronic equipment.
- **Batteries:** The EU battery regulation applies to batteries and to products that contain them, with labelling and sometimes removability or replaceability requirements. Confirm the current obligations for your product type.
- **Packaging and waste:** Producer responsibility schemes for packaging and electrical and electronic waste exist in many member states and may require registration before you sell. Requirements differ by country.
- **Food-contact materials:** Framework Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004 applies to all food-contact materials. Plastics must also comply with Regulation (EU) No 10/2011. Germany's LFGB standard is a separate national standard, and a test report under one does not automatically satisfy the other. If you sell into Germany, confirm whether LFGB testing is expected by your retailers.
- **Toys:** The EU Toy Safety Directive and the EN 71 series of tests apply. Age and warning labelling must appear on the packaging in the language required by the member state where the product is sold.
- **Textile labelling:** Fibre content labelling is required, and mislabelling can cause goods to be held.

**Member state differences.** Import VAT, packaging registration, language requirements, and some enforcement practices vary by country. If you plan to sell across multiple member states, map these requirements country by country.

### Module C: Australia

**Low-value goods.** Australia applies GST to low-value imported goods, and the rules for how it is collected depend on the value of the order and the type of seller. Confirm the current thresholds and collection method with the Australian Border Force and the Australian Taxation Office before setting prices.

**Product safety.** Check applicable Australian product safety rules, bans and mandatory standards. Some products are subject to mandatory standards, including certain children's products and furniture. Confirm whether your product is covered by a mandatory standard.

**Electrical products.** Electrical products generally need a Regulatory Compliance Mark (RCM) and compliance with the relevant Australian electrical standards. Check the requirements for your specific product type.

**Wireless products.** Radiocommunications devices are subject to the Australian Communications and Media Authority (ACMA) rules, which require compliance before supply. Confirm the applicable standard for your device.

**Biosecurity.** Timber and some plant-based products, including certain wooden furniture and materials, may require biosecurity inspection or treatment on arrival. Plan for possible delays and inspection costs, and confirm current requirements with the Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry.

**Labelling.** Country-of-origin requirements depend on the goods and applicable rules. Confirm the required format and placement for your product type.

### Module D: Middle East (Gulf region)

The Middle East is not one market. Each country has its own conformity system, so it is misleading to describe a single "Gulf standard" that covers everything. Plan separately for each country you sell into.

**Saudi Arabia.** Products are often subject to the SABER conformity platform, which requires product registration and certification depending on product category. Confirm which products are covered and whether the importer or the manufacturer must register.

**United Arab Emirates.** The UAE uses the ECAS conformity system for certain product categories. Confirm which categories are covered and the required documents.

**Other countries.** Requirements in Qatar, Kuwait, Oman, Bahrain, and other markets vary. Some use the Gulf Standardization Organization (GSO) standards and marks, but application differs by country and product.

**Language and labelling.** Arabic labelling is commonly required or expected, and some products need specific information in Arabic. Confirm the requirements for each country.

**Logistics hub.** Many importers consolidate in Jebel Ali, in Dubai, for onward shipment to other Gulf countries. Confirm whether your shipment will be cleared at the port of entry or re-exported, because the documentation and certificates required can differ.

**Seasonal and cultural factors.** Ramadan and other religious and national holidays affect working hours, clearance speed, and consumer demand. Plan for reduced working hours during these periods, and for demand changes during the season.

**Verify per country.** For each Middle East market you plan to enter, confirm the conformity system, the list of covered products, the required documentation, and the labelling rules at the time of import.

## Part 7: Risks that appear again and again

- **Certificate fraud.** Some suppliers provide reports for different products, outdated versions, or unaccredited laboratories. Verify every report with the issuing body.
- **Certificate confusion.** Shipping certification, sales certification, and food-contact testing are separate. A supplier's document for one purpose does not cover another.
- **Trader presented as factory.** A trading stall may claim to be a factory. Ask for the factory address and verify it.
- **Samples that do not match production.** Materials, finishes, and dimensions can change between sample and bulk. Approve a production sample in writing before bulk production.
- **Battery capacity overstated.** Test capacity on sample units before the pilot order.
- **Dye lot variation.** Normal in textiles. Agree on an acceptable tolerance before ordering.
- **Hidden charges under FOB terms.** Document handling, terminal, and warehouse costs should be written into the contract.
- **Intellectual property.** Copying designs, even without logos, can lead to seizure or delisting.
- **Delayed shipments during holidays.** Build buffers, as described in Part 3.
- **Marketplace rejection after customs clearance.** Clearing customs does not mean a platform will accept your listing.


## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a licence to import?**
Usually you need a registered importer or customs account, and a customs broker or a clear arrangement for clearing the goods. Requirements differ by country and product.

**Can I use my supplier's certificate for my market?**
Only if it covers your product, your market, and the correct version of the product. Verify with the issuing body.

**Is UN 38.3 the same as a sales certificate?**
No. UN 38.3 concerns the safe transport of lithium batteries. Sales certification is separate.

**Should I order one product or several?**
Start with one product, or a small range of closely related products, so you can test demand and compliance without spreading your budget too thin.

**How often should I check the rules?**
Check before each order and before each shipment, and at least once a quarter for tariff and parcel rules, which have changed frequently in 2025 and 2026.

**Can I sell without any certification?**
For some products, yes. For many, no. Confirm the requirements for your product and market first.
